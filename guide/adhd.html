Action Guide

Operational Parameters for Executive Support
Scan For

Explosive Resistance

Intense anger, explosive fits, and a complete inability to self-soothe after being denied a request, told "no," or facing a sudden boundary.
Execute Play

The Pivot & Brake

1. Validate, Then Pivot ADHD brains need to know they are heard. First, validate the feeling: "I know it's really hard to stop." Then, wrap the boundary in a choice: "Screen time is paused. Option A or Option B?"
2. External Brakes When a fit hits, stop talking. Go low-verbal. Sit near him, project an unbothered calm, and become the physical anchor his dysregulated system needs to reboot.
Core Directive

Deficient Emotional Regulation

His prefrontal cortex lacks the neurological brakes (inhibitory control) required to moderate intense feelings.
[+] Read Neurological Breakdown

Imagine driving fast and someone suddenly yanks the emergency brake. That jarring physical shock is exactly what a sudden "no" feels like to his brain.

He isn't pitching a fit to manipulate you. He is trapped in an instantaneous flood of adrenaline and disappointment, and his brain physically has not developed the "pause" button needed to stop it. He is overwhelmed, frightened by his own intensity, and desperately needs you to lend him your calm until the storm passes.

Clinical Insight: This is an "Amygdala Hijack." During a meltdown, the brain's logic center shuts down and the survival center takes over. Verbal processing is offline, meaning talking or reasoning with him actively prolongs the fit. Silence is your strongest tool.
Contingency Protocols: When the Play Fails
What if he keeps asking for the denied item over and over?
This is called "perseveration"—his ADHD brain is stuck in a thought loop and literally cannot move on. Use the "Broken Record" technique, but lead with empathy rather than dismissal. State the boundary and the pivot choice exactly once. If he repeats the demand, reply with calm validation: "I hear how much you want that, and I know it's frustrating, but my answer is still no." Do not engage in a debate loop. Calm consistency eventually breaks the loop without making him feel unheard.
What if the fit escalates into physical aggression (hitting, throwing)?
Prioritize physical safety and completely disengage verbally. Move out of striking distance or use a couch cushion to block. State clearly, exactly once: "I will keep you safe, but I will not let you hit." Give zero additional verbal or emotional feedback until his nervous system de-escalates. Reacting strongly to the hit reinforces it as an effective way to control the room.
Scan For

Rigidity Panic

Extreme distress, meltdowns, or rigid stubbornness when a planned path deviates, an expected food is swapped, or the "normal" way of doing things is broken.
Execute Play

Map & Condition

1. Visual Front-Loading Never rely on surprises or spoken warnings. Use a visual map (whiteboard, drawings) so his working memory doesn't have to hold the plan. Talk through the "what-ifs": "If X happens, our backup plan is Y."
2. Reward Flexibility Intentionally introduce micro-changes (a different plate, a new route to the store) in safe environments. Heavily praise and reward him for tolerating the pivot to build his resilience muscle.
Core Directive

Cognitive Inflexibility

His brain struggles immensely to shift gears. Strict routines serve as an essential anchor, saving him massive amounts of cognitive energy.
[+] Read Neurological Breakdown

For an ADHD brain, the internal world is often a chaotic storm of unfiltered thoughts and impulses. To manage this, he relies heavily on a predictable external world.

When a routine breaks, the ground falls out from under him. He isn't just being stubborn; he is experiencing the panic of unpredictability. Having to calculate a new plan on the fly drains his mental battery instantly. He demands "normal" because predictability equals safety.

Clinical Insight: This is compounded by ADHD "Time Blindness." Because he struggles to sense the passage of time, any transition feels sudden and abrupt. Visual maps and timers act as neurological prosthetics, externalizing the concept of time and sequence so his brain doesn't have to guess.
Contingency Protocols: When the Play Fails
What if he refuses the backup plan (Plan B) completely and shuts down?
Wait it out. The goal isn't to force him to *like* Plan B right away, but to ride out the profound disappointment of Plan A failing. Sit with him in the disappointment: "It's hard when plans change." Let the shutdown run its natural course before aggressively pushing the new activity. Forcing a pivot during a shutdown will trigger a meltdown.
What if the change is completely unexpected and we didn't have a backup plan?
Co-regulate first, logic second. Say, "Whoa, that was a surprise! I didn't see that coming either." Acknowledge the shock to validate his physical reaction. Give his brain a minute to process the sudden change of state before attempting to problem-solve or offer a solution.
Scan For

Object Dependency

Inability or refusal to perform bodily functions (pooping) without a highly specific object—namely, his designated potty seat—regardless of the actual bathroom environment.
Execute Play

Gradual Decoupling

1. Transport the Anchor If traveling, bring the exact seat. Do not attempt training in high-stress, unfamiliar public environments. It is his neurological anchor.
2. Systematic Micro-Transitions To build independence, change one variable at a time at home. Move the seat to a different toilet. Then, introduce a "travel version" of the seat at home. Never force a bare toilet in public until he masters a bare toilet in his safe home.
Core Directive

Autonomic Lockdown

Physical release requires absolute neurological safety. His brain has inextricably mapped "safety" directly to the physical sensation of that specific seat.
[+] Read Neurological Breakdown

Going to the bathroom leaves a mammal incredibly vulnerable. Over time, his brain learned that the specific pressure, shape, and familiarity of his potty seat means "we are safe from danger."

Without that specific physical input, his nervous system sends a blaring red-alert signal to his body, literally clinching his autonomic muscles. He couldn't go even if he wanted to. It is a biological lockdown, an involuntary defense mechanism, not a behavioral act of defiance.

Clinical Insight: This relies on "State-Dependent Learning." His brain cannot generalize the skill of using a toilet until the new environment perfectly mimics the original, safe physical state. Pushing too fast increases his "allostatic load" (chronic stress), which actively prevents muscle relaxation.
Contingency Protocols: When the Play Fails
What if we forgot the travel seat and he is holding it or in pain?
Do not force the bare toilet during a high-stress moment—this will only increase his anxiety and resistance toward new bathrooms. Instead, focus on providing physical stability to mimic the feeling of his safe seat. You can hold him securely around the waist or let him brace his feet on your legs for grounding. If the panic is too high and he cannot release, abort the mission and head home or to a familiar safe place. Preventing painful holding and severe constipation is the priority. Emotional and physical health always overrides the convenience of a public restroom.
What if he refuses to even look at the "travel seat" at home during micro-transitions?
Dial back the difficulty. If he won't sit on the new seat, have the travel seat just sit *next* to the toilet while he uses his normal seat. Reward that passive visual exposure. The next week, have him touch it with his hand. In occupational therapy, these micro-transitions must be truly microscopic to avoid triggering the threat response.
Scan For

Sensory Rejection

Extreme physical aversions to stimuli: gagging at the dentist, refusing the unpredictable textures of new foods, or panicking under bright lights or noise.
Execute Play

Input Management

1. Zero-Pressure Data Collection For new foods, utilize the SOS feeding approach. Remove the demand to consume. Frame it as gathering data. Touch it, smell it, tap it to the lips. The goal is exposure, not completion.
2. Proprioceptive Reset Before entering a high-sensory environment (dentist), have him do "heavy work"—pushing a wall, carrying a heavy basket. Deep muscle pressure grounds the nervous system.
Core Directive

Sensory Gating Failure

His executive function struggles to filter out irrelevant sensory data, causing a literal "fight or flight" overload response in his nervous system.
[+] Read Neurological Breakdown

Right now, your brain is seamlessly filtering out background noise, the feeling of your clothing, and the brightness of the lights so you can read this text. His brain struggles to do this automatically.

The dentist's light isn't just bright; it's blinding. The metallic taste isn't just weird; it's agonizing. Every piece of sensory data hits his working memory at maximum intensity simultaneously. Gagging isn't him being dramatic; it is his body's desperate, physical attempt to reject painful sensory input.

Clinical Insight: Deep proprioceptive input (heavy work, weighted blankets) provides organizing feedback to the central nervous system. It physically down-regulates the sympathetic nervous system (fight or flight) and boosts dopamine and serotonin, acting as a natural buffer against sensory pain.
Contingency Protocols: When the Play Fails
What if the "heavy work" didn't help and he goes into a full meltdown at the dentist?
Abort the mission. A sensory meltdown is a severe neurological event, not bad behavior. Forcing compliance during a meltdown reinforces a massive stress response and ensures the next visit will be much harder to tolerate. Stop the procedure, soothe him, and discuss mild sedation options or desensitization visits with the provider for the next attempt.
What if he gags or vomits during the "data collection" phase of a new food?
Maintain a perfectly neutral reaction. Clean it up without frustration, anger, or pity. Say, "Data logged. That texture is a 'no' for right now." End the exposure immediately and offer a safe, highly predictable food. Do not make the involuntary physical reaction a point of shame or behavioral correction.
